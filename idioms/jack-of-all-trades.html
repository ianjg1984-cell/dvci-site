Jack of All Trades, Master of None

"Someone reasonably capable at many different things, but not a true expert in any single one."

Share on Facebook

"Jack" was such a common nickname for "John" in medieval England that it became a stand-in term for an anonymous, everyday man, the same instinct behind words like "jack-of-the-clock." "Jack of all trades" as a phrase is recorded from 1612, in Geffray Minshull's account of his time in prison, and it originally carried no insult at all, simply describing someone broadly, usefully skilled. The stinging second half, "master of none," is a much later addition, generally dated to the 18th century, and it's this addition that flipped the phrase from a compliment into a put-down.

Myth vs. FactPeople often assume the put-down was baked in from the start. It wasn't. For roughly a century and a half, being a 'jack of all trades' was simply a nice thing to be called.
Sources: Geffray Minshull, Essayes and Characters of a Prison and Prisoners (1612); 'master of none' addition dated to the 18th century per etymological sources.
