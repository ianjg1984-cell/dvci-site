Sailing & the Sea Likely Origin (Disputed)

Round Robin

"Something done in rotation, or a petition signed in a circle so no signer stands out as leader."

Share on Facebook

A petition signed in a circle so that no signatory could be singled out as the ringleader, a trick associated with sailors protesting to their officers. The earliest citation for that sense on Wiktionary is Charles Johnson's A General History of the Pyrates (second edition, 1724), which describes a petition drawn up "in the Manner of what they call a Round Robin." Where "robin" comes from is the weak point. One suggestion is that it corrupts the French ruban rond, "round ribbon," from 17th-century France, where petitions were supposedly signed on ribbons attached in a circle, but Wiktionary notes that this doesn't explain the term's other, unrelated senses and gives the main etymology as simply "round" plus "robin." Wikipedia flags the sailors-and-French-ribbon story as needing a citation, and the earliest attested sense of the term is actually something else entirely: a consecrated host, recorded in 1659.

Sources: Charles Johnson, A General History of the Pyrates (2nd ed., 1724), as cited on Wiktionary; Thomas Fuller, The Appeal of Iniured Innocence (1659); Wikipedia's 'Round-robin (document)' article.
