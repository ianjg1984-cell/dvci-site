Food & Dining Popular Myth

A Square Meal

"A full, satisfying, proper meal."

Share on Facebook

The popular story is that "square meal" comes from the square wooden plates once used in the Royal Navy. Grammarphobia's verdict: "Forget about it." Quick and Dirty Tips adds that neither The Sailor's Word Book nor the Oxford English Dictionary connects the phrase to the navy. The likelier source is the adjective "square" meaning fair, just, or honest, which the Oxford English Dictionary traces to Robert Greene's The Defence of Conny Catching (1592) and which also gave us "fair and square." The phrase itself is American. The OED's earliest example is from All the Year Round, the magazine edited by Charles Dickens, on 19 September 1868: "'square meals': by which is meant full meals." Earlier examples turn up online, including an advertisement in the Mountain Democrat of Placerville, California, on 8 November 1856 for a "square meal" at the Hope and Neptune, and a letter in the Georgetown News of 7 February 1856 that tells readers to "get a square meal."

Myth vs. FactThe Royal Navy square-plates story is repeated as fact, but there's no evidence connecting the phrase to naval dining. 'Square' meaning fair or honest is the likelier source.
Sources: Grammarphobia (21 June 2019) on the OED's 1868 citation and the 1856 Placerville advertisement; Quick and Dirty Tips on the 1856 Georgetown News letter and the lack of any naval link; Robert Greene, The Defence of Conny Catching (1592).
