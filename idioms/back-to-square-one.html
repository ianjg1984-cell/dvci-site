Back to Square One

"Back at the very beginning, having made no progress."

Share on Facebook

The most repeated story is that it comes from early BBC radio football commentary. Phrase Finder confirms the real background: in January 1927 The Radio Times referred to a numbered grid of squares printed so listeners could follow the commentary. But Phrase Finder points out that at no point in any existing commentary is the phrase "back to square one" used, and a discussion on Wiktionary adds that the system was dropped soon after it began. The earliest print use Phrase Finder knows of is from the Economic Journal in 1952, which specifically cites snakes and ladders, with a second example in the Liverpool Echo in November 1959. Phrase Finder concludes that the snakes and ladders derivation has the strongest claim, though the true origin remains unproven, and hopscotch is another suggestion.

Myth vs. FactThe football-commentary story is told as fact, even by the BBC, but no surviving commentary uses the phrase and it isn't recorded in print until 1952. Snakes and ladders is the better-supported guess.
Sources: Phrase Finder, 'Back to square one'; Economic Journal (1952); Liverpool Echo (November 1959); The Radio Times (January 1927) on the BBC commentary grid.
