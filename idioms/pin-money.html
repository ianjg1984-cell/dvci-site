Pin Money

"A small amount of money for incidentals; originally an allowance for a wife's personal expenses."

Share on Facebook

Not originally small change. "Pin money" was an allowance, often provided for in a marriage contract, that a husband gave his wife (or a father his daughter) for personal expenses; Wiktionary dates that sense from the 16th century. The editors of Merriam-Webster, answering a reader in 1996, noted that in the 17th and 18th centuries, when "pin money" was a legal term, the sum was often written into a marriage contract, and gave an example of a 16th-century father allowing his daughter the income from the lease of a parsonage to "by her pynnes." The pins in question are presumably the ones used to fasten clothing, once a notable expense; the same article quotes a proverb of the time, "a pin a day is a groat a year." One often-cited allowance, that of the princess of Orange, was allegedly 50,000 pounds a year. The meaning later shrank to a small sum for incidentals. Wiktionary's earliest dated quotation is from Charles Walker's 1723 Memoirs of Sally Salisbury: "Shall you wear such Things, and I want Pin-Money?"

Sources: Merriam-Webster editors, quoted in the Deseret News (30 June 1996); Wiktionary's entry for 'pin money,' including its 1723 quotation from Charles Walker's Memoirs of Sally Salisbury.
