Sailing & the Sea Likely Origin (Disputed)

To the Bitter End

"To the very last, however grim or difficult it gets."

Share on Facebook

The usual explanation is nautical. On a sailing ship, the "bitter end" was the inboard end of the anchor cable, fastened to the bitts, the sturdy deck posts used to secure lines; if the windlass brake slipped in a storm and the cable ran out, it could reach "the bitter end." "Bitter" in this nautical sense is recorded in Captain John Smith's Seaman's Grammar (1627), and Wikipedia's article on the anchor windlass states flatly that this is the origin of "to the bitter end." World Wide Words is more cautious. It calls the nautical account "almost universally given," says there is enough evidence to cast some doubt on it, and notes that the Oxford English Dictionary regards the phrase's history as uncertain. It suggests the figurative sense may owe something to the Bible, where Proverbs 5 in the 1611 King James translation describes an end "bitter as wormwood," and points out that the phrase wasn't only a sailor's expression.

Myth vs. FactThe anchor-cable story is often stated as fact, but the Oxford English Dictionary describes the history as uncertain, and World Wide Words argues a biblical influence on the figurative sense is also possible.
Sources: Captain John Smith, Seaman's Grammar (1627); Wikipedia's 'Anchor windlass' article; World Wide Words, noting the OED regards the history as uncertain.
