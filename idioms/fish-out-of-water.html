A Fish Out of Water

"Someone who feels awkward or completely out of place in an unfamiliar situation."

Share on Facebook

One of the oldest idioms still in common English use. Geoffrey Chaucer used a version of the image in the general prologue to The Canterbury Tales (written 1387-1400), describing a monk outside his monastery as being "like to a fish that is waterless." The more familiar modern wording appears later, in Samuel Purchas's travel writing Pilgrimage (1613): "The Arabians out of the desarts are as Fishes out of the Water." The metaphor has stayed essentially unchanged for over six centuries because the image is so immediately understandable.

Sources: Geoffrey Chaucer, The Canterbury Tales, General Prologue (c. 1387-1400); Samuel Purchas, Pilgrimage (1613).
