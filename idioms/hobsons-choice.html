Hobson's Choice

"A choice that is really no choice at all: take what's offered or nothing."

Share on Facebook

The traditional story centres on Thomas Hobson (c. 1544 to 1631), a livery stable owner in Cambridge who is said to have offered customers either the horse in the stall nearest the door or none at all, supposedly so the best horses wouldn't be overworked. A plaque beneath a painting of Hobson at Cambridge Guildhall repeats the detail, and Milton helped make both Hobson and the phrase well known by mocking him in several mock epitaphs. The Oxford English Dictionary's earliest written example, though, is Samuel Fisher's The rustick's alarm to the Rabbies (1660), which says "choose whether you will have this or none" and calls it "the Proverb," without mentioning Hobson, stables, or horses. Joseph Addison later wrote in The Spectator (1712) that it became a proverb from Hobson. So the stable story is the traditional explanation, but the earliest surviving use doesn't actually tie the saying to him.

Myth vs. FactThe stable-door story is usually told as settled fact, but the earliest written use (1660) never mentions Hobson or horses, and the link to him only appears in later accounts. Treat it as a traditional explanation rather than proven history.
Sources: Samuel Fisher, The rustick's alarm to the Rabbies (1660), the Oxford English Dictionary's earliest citation; Joseph Addison, The Spectator (1712); Hobson's dates and the stable story as documented on Wikipedia.
